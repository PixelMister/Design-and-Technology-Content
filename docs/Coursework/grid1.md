# Grid 1: Identifying and Outlining Possibilties for design

## Identification and Investigation of Design Possibilities

- *Maximum marks: 9*
- *Indicative portfolio space: approximately 1–3 A3 sides*

**What are you trying to achieve?**
You need to **identify, investigate and justify** a genuine design opportunity that could lead to the development of a new product.

This means demonstrating that your chosen project is based on an **identifiable problem or need** rather than simply something you would like to manufacture.

Your investigation should be sufficiently detailed to show that you have considered possible opportunities, understood the situation and selected a suitable area to develop.

## 1.1 Identify a suitable design context
Begin by identifying a context in which a design opportunity might exist.

A design context is the situation or environment within which your product will be used.

Suitable starting points might include:

- A person who has difficulty completing an everyday activity.
- A business requiring a display, storage or organisational solution.
- A group of people who experience problems with existing products.
- An opportunity to improve the usability of a particular product.
- A problem involving limited space, movement, accessibility or storage.
- An identified demand for a new product within a market.
- A practical problem within a school, community organisation or workplace.

You should select a context that allows you to explore a range of solutions.

!!! example "Exploring a Context"

    For example, rather than deciding immediately to manufacture a folding chair, you could investigate the wider problem of seating and space management in compact environments.

    This gives you the opportunity to explore different approaches before committing to a particular product.

## 1.2 Identify and establish a relationship with your client
A client should provide meaningful information about the problem and help inform your design decisions.

Your client might be an individual, a business, an organisation or representatives of a target market.

You should establish:
- Who the client is.
- Their relationship to the design problem.
- Their expectations and priorities.
- The intended users of the product.
- Their willingness to provide feedback during the project.

If the client is different from the intended user, explain this clearly.

!!! example "Example"
    a business owner might commission a display unit, but customers and employees may be the people who interact with it.

## 1.3 Investigate the client's needs, wants and values
The delivery guide places considerable importance on understanding the client.

You must establish more than simply what the client says they want as understanding the difference allows you to propose alternative solutions.

=== "Needs" 
    These are the essential requirements that must be satisfied.

    For example:
    -   *A seating product must support the intended user.*
    -   *A storage product must accommodate particular objects.*
    -   *A portable product must be manageable to transport.*

=== "Wants" 
    These are desirable features that may improve the product.
    
    For example:
    - *A particular aesthetic style.*
    - *Additional storage.*
    - *A choice of finishes.*
    - *A compact or folding mechanism.*

=== "Values" 

    These are the client's wider priorities or beliefs.

    For example:
    - *Sustainability.*
    - *Durability.*
    - *Affordability.*
    - *Accessibility.*
    - *Repairability.*
    - *Responsible material sourcing.*

    A client may want a particular material because they like its appearance, but their underlying need may be for a lightweight, durable product.

## 1.4 Carry out an initial client investigation

Plan an interview, questionnaire or structured discussion.
Begin with open questions that encourage detailed responses.

---

For example:
-	Can you explain the main problem you currently experience?
-	When does the problem occur?
-	How frequently does it affect you?
-	How do you currently manage the situation?
-	What is most frustrating about the existing solution?
-	Which features are essential?
-	Which features would be desirable but not essential?
-	What would make the greatest improvement?
-	Are there any limitations involving space, budget, safety or accessibility?

---

Use follow-up questions to clarify responses.

If a client says that a product must be small, ask what they mean by small.

If they say it must be strong, establish what loads or conditions it must withstand.

If they say it must be comfortable, investigate which aspects of comfort are most important.

Do not accept vague requirements without investigating what they mean.

## 1.5 Analyse the information you have collected

After gathering client information, identify the most important findings.

You should not simply present a transcript or list of questionnaire responses.

Explain what the information suggests about the design problem.

For example:

- **Client finding:** Existing seating takes up too much room when it is not being used.
- **Analysis:** Storage volume is an important consideration. A solution that folds, stacks or can be dismantled may provide advantages over conventional fixed seating.
- **Design implication:** Investigate space-saving mechanisms and compare their suitability for the environment.

This approach demonstrates how investigation leads to informed decisions.

## 1.6 Narrow down your design possibilities

Use your research to identify the most appropriate area of focus.

**Consider:**
-	The importance of the problem.
-	The needs of the intended user.
-	The opportunity for different design approaches.
-	The complexity of the product.
-	The materials and processes involved.
-	The opportunity to demonstrate advanced manufacturing skills.
-	Whether the product can be developed into a suitable working prototype.
-	The time and resources available.

**You Should:**
- Compare several opportunities against relevant criteria.
- Not simply choose the easiest project.
- Select an opportunity that is meaningful, sufficiently challenging and realistic.

## 1.7 Justify your chosen design possibility

Your justification should explain why your chosen opportunity is appropriate.

**Include:**
1.	The problem you have identified.
2.	Evidence that the problem exists.
3.	Who is affected and how.
4.	Why existing solutions are insufficient.
5.	The most important client requirements.
6.	The potential benefits of developing a new product.
7.	Why the opportunity is appropriate for an A-Level project.

!!! example "Example of Analytical Writing"

    "The investigation identified that existing seating arrangements create difficulties when moving around the narrowboat and storing equipment. The client prioritised comfortable seating but also identified the need to reduce storage space when the seating is not in use. This suggests that the design opportunity should focus on developing a compact seating solution with an appropriate balance of stability, comfort and portability. Further investigation into folding mechanisms, anthropometric dimensions and suitable materials will therefore be required."

This is an illustrative example. Your own justification must be based on your actual investigation.

**What does high-quality evidence look like?**

For this assessment area, strong work demonstrates:
-	In-depth investigation of relevant possibilities.
-	A well-established client or user context.
-	Comprehensive consideration of needs, wants and values.
-	A focused and justified design opportunity.
-	Clear evidence that the chosen problem has been informed by investigation.

**Common weaknesses to avoid**
-	Deciding what you will make before investigating the problem.
-	Choosing a project because it seems easy.
-	Using a client simply to approve your ideas.
-	Asking only yes/no questions.
-	Presenting interview responses without analysis.
-	Failing to investigate alternative design opportunities.
-	Providing a justification based entirely on personal preference.
