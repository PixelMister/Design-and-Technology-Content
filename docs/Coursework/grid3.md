# Grid 3: Design Brief and Specification

## Design Brief and Specification
- *Maximum marks: 9*
- *Indicative portfolio space: approximately 1–2 A3 sides*

**What are you trying to achieve?**

You must convert your investigation into a clear statement of the design problem and a detailed set of requirements that will guide your project.

The design brief explains what needs to be achieved.

The specification explains the requirements that the successful product must satisfy.

## 3.1 Write your refined design brief

Your design brief should be based on the findings of Grid 1 and 2.

**It should:**
-	Identify the client and intended users.
-	Explain the existing problem.
-	Describe the intended purpose of the product.
-	Identify the context of use.
-	Summarise important user requirements.
-	Establish what the project intends to achieve.

Avoid describing the final solution in unnecessary detail at this stage.

Your brief should leave scope for different design approaches.

!!! example "Writing Framework"
    I intend to design and manufacture a [type of product or solution] for [client/user] to address [identified problem]. The product will be used in [context] and must consider [key requirements]. The design will need to balance [important competing considerations] while providing a practical and appropriate solution.

This is a writing framework, not a brief to copy. Replace it with a statement specific to your investigation.

## 3.2 Organise your specification

A detailed specification could include the following categories:
1.	Purpose and function.
2.	User requirements.
3.	Ergonomics and anthropometrics.
4.	Form and aesthetics.
5.	Materials and components.
6.	Performance.
7.	Safety and standards.
8.	Manufacturing.
9.	Sustainability.
10.	Cost and production.

Not every category requires the same number of statements.
Focus on the requirements relevant to your design opportunity.

## 3.3 Make each requirement specific

Avoid vague statements.

!!! example "Example 1"

    === "Weak"
        The product must be strong.

    === "Improved"
        The product must withstand the intended service load without unsafe deformation or failure, with the load and acceptance criteria established through relevant research.

!!! example "Example 2"

    === "Weak"
        The product must be comfortable.

    === "Improved"
        The seating dimensions and support geometry must be informed by anthropometric information and assessed through trials involving intended users.

!!! example "Example 3"

    === "Weak"
        The product must be small.

    === "Improved"
        When stored, the product must fit within the available space measured during the client investigation.

The final version should include specific verified measurements wherever possible.

## 3.4 Justify every specification point

Each requirement should link back to the research.

A useful format is:

``` mermaid
graph LR
  A[Requirement] --> B[Evidence or Reason] --> C[Proposed Method of Testing]

```

!!! example "Example Justification"
    | Requirement | Justification | How It Could Be Tested |
    |---|---|---|
    | **The product must fit within the available storage space.** | The client identified restricted storage as a major issue. | Measure the completed prototype in its storage configuration. |
    | **The product must use materials appropriate for the intended environment.** | The product may encounter moisture during use. | Assess material and finish suitability using relevant data and appropriate tests. |
    | **The product must be stable during normal use.** | The user requires a safe and reliable product. | Carry out an appropriate stability test under controlled conditions. |

This creates a clear connection between research and final evaluation.

## 3.5 Prioritise essential and desirable requirements

You may find that some requirements conflict.

**For example:**
•	Increasing strength may increase mass.
•	Improving durability may increase cost.
•	Adding adjustability may increase complexity.
•	Reducing material usage may affect rigidity.

Identify which requirements are essential and which are desirable.

This will help you make reasoned compromises later.

## 3.6 Consider performance requirements

Performance requirements are particularly important because they provide evidence of the product's technical suitability.

Depending on your design, you might investigate:
-	Load capacity.
-	Deflection.
-	Stability.
-	Operating force.
-	Accuracy.
-	Speed of operation.
-	Electrical performance.
-	Resistance to moisture.
-	Durability.
-	Maintenance requirements.
Use appropriate technical research to establish realistic values.

## 3.7 Check your specification with your client

Present your brief and specification to your client.

**Ask:**
-	Does the brief reflect the problem accurately?
-	Are the most important requirements included?
-	Have I misunderstood anything?
-	Are any requirements unnecessary?
-	Are there any missing priorities?

Record significant feedback and any resulting amendments.

## 3.8 Create a specification tracking system

Give each specification point a reference code.

!!! example "Specification Codes"
    -	F1: Function.
    -	E1: Ergonomics.
    -	M1: Materials.
    -	S1: Safety.
    -	C1: Cost.

Use these references later when explaining how your design ideas meet the specification.

This is a useful organisational technique rather than a requirement to use a particular coding system.

**Common weaknesses to avoid**
-	Writing a brief that already dictates the complete solution.
-	Producing a specification containing only short, vague statements.
-	Failing to justify requirements.
-	Including targets that cannot be tested.
-	Inventing technical values without research.
-	Forgetting the client's priorities.
-	Failing to consider manufacture, cost and production scale.
