# Grid 4: DESIGN IDEAS

## Design Ideas
- *Maximum marks: 9*
- *Indicative portfolio space: approximately 4–6 A3 sides*

**What are you trying to achieve?**

You need to generate realistic, workable and creative ideas that address the needs of your client and the requirements of your specification.

Your ideas should explore different approaches rather than presenting minor variations of one solution.

## 4.1 Begin with your specification

Before drawing, review the most important design requirements.

*Identify:*
-	Essential functions.
-	Ergonomic requirements.
-	Performance expectations.
-	Dimensional restrictions.
-	Material considerations.
-	Aesthetic preferences.
-	Manufacturing limitations.

You should be able to explain which requirements each idea addresses.

## 4.2 Explore different design strategies

The delivery guide expects you to use appropriate design strategies.

Possible approaches include:
- Brainstorming
- Generate a wide range of possible features and approaches without initially evaluating them.
- Morphological analysis

Break the product into its main functions and explore alternative ways of achieving each function.

!!! example "Design Strategies Analysis"
    | Function | Approach A | Approach B | Approach C |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | **Support** | Fixed frame | Folding frame | Modular structure |
    | **Storage** | Stackable | Foldable | Demountable |
    | **Adjustment** | Fixed dimensions | Telescopic system | Multiple positions |
    | **Construction** | Mechanical fixings | Interlocking components | Permanent joints |

You can combine compatible approaches to produce different concepts.

**Biomimicry**

Investigate forms or structures found in nature where they provide useful inspiration.
You must explain the functional relationship rather than simply copying an organic shape.

**SCAMPER**

Consider whether you can:
- Substitute.
-	Combine.
-	Adapt.
-	Modify.
-	Put to another use.
-	Eliminate.
-	Reverse or rearrange.

**Historical and cultural research**

Investigate relevant design movements, historical products or cultural influences.

Consider how their characteristics could influence form, construction or user experience.

## 4.3 Generate genuinely different design proposals

Aim to explore several distinct concepts.

For example, in a space-saving seating project:
-	One concept might investigate a folding frame.
-	Another might investigate nesting or stacking.
-	Another might explore modular components.
-	Another might investigate a dual-function product.

These approaches respond to the same problem in different ways.
The important factor is the range and quality of design exploration, not producing an arbitrary number of sketches.

## 4.4 Communicate the design's function

Your drawings should show how the proposed product works.

**Consider including:**
-	Different positions of moving components.
-	Exploded views.
-	Component relationships.
-	Mechanism diagrams.
-	User interaction diagrams.
-	Storage and operating configurations.

A visually attractive drawing is not sufficient if the mechanism or construction cannot be understood.

## 4.5 Add technical annotations

**For each idea, explain:**
-	Which requirements it addresses.
-	How it would function.
-	Which materials may be suitable.
-	Which manufacturing methods could be used.
-	What benefits it offers the user.
-	What disadvantages or risks exist.
-	What further investigation would be needed.

!!! example "Example Annotation"
    The folding support could reduce storage volume and address requirement F2. However, the hinge position will influence both stability and the complexity of assembly. Further modelling is needed to establish an appropriate location and determine whether additional bracing is required.

    This is much more effective than writing "folding design saves space".

## 4.6 Demonstrate material and process understanding

Even during initial design generation, your proposals should show that you understand how products are manufactured.

**Consider:**
-	Material thickness.
-	Joining methods.
-	Component geometry.
-	Structural support.
-	Standard fittings.
-	Assembly sequence.
-	Access for tools.
-	Manufacturing complexity.

You are not expected to have every detail resolved at this stage, but your ideas should be technically plausible.

## 4.7 Consider aesthetics and design influences

**Investigate relevant visual characteristics, including:**
-	Form and proportion.
-	Surface finish.
-	Colour.
-	Texture.
-	Design movements.
-	Cultural or historical influences.
-	Suitability for the intended environment.

Your aesthetic decisions should connect to the client and product context.

## 4.8 Gather initial client feedback

Present your ideas to your client.

**Ask them to consider:**
-	Function.
-	Usability.
-	Appearance.
-	Size.
-	Practicality.
-	Suitability for the intended environment.

Ask for reasons behind their opinions.

Record both positive and negative feedback.

## 4.9 Review your initial ideas

Conclude by identifying which ideas or features show the greatest potential.

Do not simply choose the concept that looks best.

Consider technical feasibility, user requirements, manufacturing potential and feedback.

You may decide to combine successful features from different concepts.

**Common weaknesses to avoid**
-	Producing several almost identical designs.
-	Creating concepts unrelated to the specification.
-	Focusing entirely on appearance.
-	Using unexplained materials or manufacturing methods.
-	Presenting decorative annotations rather than technical explanations.
-	Selecting a preferred idea without evaluation.
