# The Complete Independent Study Handbook A step-by-step guide to achieving your potential in the Non-Examined Assessment (NEA)
## INTRODUCTION: YOUR NEA PROJECT
Your Non-Examined Assessment (NEA) is one of the most important parts of your A-Level Design and Technology qualification. It provides an opportunity to demonstrate your ability to work as a designer by identifying a genuine problem, investigating the needs of a client, developing an appropriate design solution, manufacturing a prototype and evaluating the success of your work.

Unlike a written examination, the NEA allows you to demonstrate your understanding through an extended, practical project. You will need to combine the knowledge you have gained from studying Design and Technology with your own creativity, technical understanding, manufacturing skills and ability to make informed decisions.
Your NEA contributes 50% of your final A-Level grade and is assessed out of 120 marks.

The project is divided into four main parts, containing eleven assessment areas.

Remember that all of these assessment areas are connected. For example, your research should inform your specification, your specification should influence your designs, and your final evaluation should establish how successfully your prototype meets those original requirements.
 
What does a successful NEA look like?
A successful NEA is not simply a collection of attractive drawings or photographs of a finished product.
It is a carefully documented design process that demonstrates:
Investigation	You have identified and explored a genuine design opportunity.
Understanding	You recognise the needs, wants and values of the people for whom you are designing.
Research	You can investigate relevant information and use it to inform your decisions
Creativity	You can generate a range of appropriate and imaginative design solutions.
Technical Knowledge	You understand the materials, components and manufacturing processes involved.
Development: 	You can model, test, refine and improve your ideas.
Justification: 	You can explain why particular decisions were made.
Manufacturing: 	You can use tools, equipment and practical techniques safely and accurately.
Evaluation: 	You can test your prototype, analyse the results and reach balanced conclusions.

At every stage, you should be able to answer five questions:
 
1.	What have I done?
2.	Why have I done it?
3.	What have I discovered or learnt?
4.	How has this influenced my design?
5.	What needs to happen next?
 
These questions should guide the development of your portfolio throughout the project.
 
Understanding the difference between describing, analysing, evaluating and justifying
One of the most important skills at A-Level is recognising the difference between simply describing something and demonstrating deeper understanding.

Skill	What it means	Example
Describe	State what something is or what happened.	The frame is manufactured from aluminium.
Explain	Give a reason for a feature or decision.	Aluminium was selected because it is lightweight and corrosion-resistant.
Analyse	Examine the implications and relationships between different factors.	Using aluminium reduces the product's mass, but its cost, stiffness and joining requirements must be considered alongside the intended function.
Evaluate	Judge how successful or suitable something is using evidence.	Aluminium appears appropriate for the frame because the prototype remained sufficiently rigid during testing, although an alternative material may reduce production cost.
Justify	Defend a decision using relevant evidence.	Aluminium was selected over mild steel because the client identified portability as a priority, and comparative testing demonstrated that the chosen section provided sufficient stiffness at a lower mass.
Your aim throughout the NEA should be to move beyond description and demonstrate analysis, evaluation and justification wherever appropriate.
How much work should you produce?
The Pearson delivery guide suggests a portfolio of approximately 20–40 sides of A3 paper, or an electronic equivalent, alongside your models and final prototype. This is purely exam guidance, and even the exemplars that the exam board provides, far surpass this advisory.
This is not a requirement to fill every available page. The quality of your work is more important than its quantity.
A portfolio with clear drawings, relevant evidence and well-justified decisions is more effective than a much larger portfolio containing repeated information, unnecessary photographs or research that has not influenced the design.
The guide provides indicative page ranges for certain assessment areas. These are useful for planning, but they are not fixed requirements or individual page quotas. Evidence can contribute to more than one assessment area.
An important note about independent study
You are responsible for managing your project and making your own design decisions. However, your NEA is subject to assessment controls. Your final prototype must be produced under the required supervision, and your teacher will explain which activities can be undertaken independently and which must take place in controlled conditions.
Use independent study time for permitted activities such as research, analysis, planning, design development, portfolio organisation and reflection. Always follow your teacher's instructions regarding assessed work, workshop access and the use of assistance.
