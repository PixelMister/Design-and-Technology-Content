# Grid 2: Investigation of Needs and Research

## Investigation of Needs and Research
- *Maximum marks: 15*
- *Indicative portfolio space: approximately 4–5 A3 sides*

**What are you trying to achieve?**

You must investigate the design problem comprehensively and select relevant research that informs the development of your prototype.

This section is not about collecting as much information as possible.

It is about identifying what you need to know, locating useful evidence and demonstrating how your findings influence the design.

## 2.1 Begin with a detailed problem analysis

Before researching, break the problem into smaller areas.

Consider the factors that could influence the design.

| Design Consideration | Questions to Consider |
|---|---|
| **Function** | • What must the product do?<br>• What tasks must it perform?<br>• Does it need to move, fold, rotate or adjust?<br>• What level of performance is required?<br>• What could prevent it from functioning successfully? |
| **Form** | • What physical characteristics might be appropriate?<br>• Are there limitations involving size or shape?<br>• How might proportions affect usability?<br>• What appearance would suit the intended context? |
| **Ergonomics** | • How will users interact with the product?<br>• Will it need handles, controls or seating surfaces?<br>• Are comfort, posture or grip important?<br>• Are accessibility considerations relevant? |
| **Materials** | • What material properties might be necessary?<br>• Does the product need to be strong, lightweight, flexible or durable?<br>• Will it be exposed to water, heat, sunlight or impact? |
| **Manufacture** | • What processes may be suitable?<br>• How could components be joined?<br>• What level of production is intended?<br>• How might manufacturing affect cost? |
| **Safety** | • Could the design introduce hazards during use?<br>• Are there relevant standards or guidance?<br>• What failures would create the greatest risk? |
| **Sustainability** | • How could the environmental impact be reduced?<br>• Could materials be responsibly sourced?<br>• Could the product be repaired or recycled? |
| **Cost** | • What budget is available?<br>• What materials or processes could make the product expensive?<br>• Is the intended solution commercially realistic? |

Your problem analysis should help you determine your research priorities.

## 2.2 Plan your research

Create a research plan explaining what you need to investigate and why.

| Research Area | Question to Investigate | Possible Source | How It May Influence the Design |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Existing Products** | How do competing products solve the same problem? | Manufacturers, product testing, shops | Identify useful features and limitations. |
| **Ergonomics** | What dimensions support comfortable use? | Anthropometric data, user trials | Establish appropriate proportions. |
| **Materials** | Which materials provide the required properties? | Datasheets, testing, suppliers | Inform material selection. |
| **Manufacture** | Which processes are appropriate? | Technical references, workshop trials | Determine feasible construction. |
| **Sustainability** | What are the main life-cycle impacts? | Material data and environmental sources | Inform sourcing and product longevity. |
| **Cost** | What would suitable materials and components cost? | Supplier information | Establish a realistic budget. |


## 2.3 Use primary and secondary research appropriately

**Primary research**
Primary research is information you gather directly.

Examples include:
-	Client interviews.
-	User questionnaires.
-	Photographs of the environment.
-	Measurements of available space.
-	Observations of users completing tasks.
-	Testing existing products.
-	Material experiments.
-	Ergonomic trials.

!!! example "Example"

    measuring the available storage space is more useful than guessing how much space the client has.

Primary research is particularly valuable when you need information specific to your design context.

**Secondary research**

Secondary research involves analysing information collected or published by others.

**Examples include:**
-	Manufacturer specifications.
-	Material datasheets.
-	Technical references.
-	Recognised standards.
-	Product reviews.
-	Published anthropometric information.
-	Research into manufacturing processes.

You should use reliable, relevant sources and record where information has come from.

!!! danger "Do Not!"
    automatically assume that promotional claims made by manufacturers are objective.

## 2.4 Carry out detailed existing product analysis

Existing product research should help you understand how other designers have addressed similar problems.

Select products that demonstrate different approaches to function, construction or appearance.

For each product, investigate the following.

| Analysis Area | What to Investigate |
|---|---|
| **Product Overview** | • Product name.<br>• Manufacturer.<br>• Intended market.<br>• Main function.<br>• Approximate price.<br>• Intended user. |
| **Functional Analysis** | • How the product works.<br>• How the user interacts with it.<br>• Whether it has moving components.<br>• How effectively it achieves its purpose.<br>• Any obvious limitations. |
| **Material Analysis** | • Materials used for important components.<br>• Relevant material properties.<br>• Why the manufacturer may have selected them.<br>• Potential alternatives. |
| **Manufacturing Analysis** | • Likely manufacturing processes.<br>• Joining methods.<br>• Component complexity.<br>• Assembly requirements.<br>• Suitability for the scale of production. |
| **Ergonomic Analysis** | • Dimensions.<br>• Grip or seating comfort.<br>• Ease of operation.<br>• Accessibility.<br>• User interaction. |
| **Aesthetic Analysis** | • Shape and proportions.<br>• Colour and finish.<br>• Design influences.<br>• Suitability for the target market. |
| **Environmental Analysis** | • Materials.<br>• Durability.<br>• Repairability.<br>• Likely manufacturing impacts.<br>• Potential for recycling. |

## 2.5 Move beyond descriptive product research

A strong product analysis explains what you can learn from the product.

**Use the following sequence:**

``` mermaid
graph LR
  A[Identify a feature] --> B[Explain its purpose] --> C[Analyse its effectiveness] --> D[Consider its suitability for your design] --> E[Identify a decision or further investigation.]

```

!!! example "Phasellus posuere in sem ut cursus"

    The product incorporates a hinged support, allowing the frame to fold into a smaller storage volume. This is relevant because the client has identified limited storage space as a major problem. However, the hinge may introduce additional movement or wear over time. I will therefore investigate suitable hinge mechanisms and test whether a folding structure can maintain sufficient stability.

This demonstrates direct application of research.

## 2.6 Research ergonomics and anthropometrics

Ergonomics concerns the relationship between people and products, including comfort, usability, safety and efficiency.

Anthropometrics concerns human body measurements.

Investigate the measurements and interactions relevant to your specific product.

**These might include:**
-	Seating height.
-	Shoulder width.
-	Reach distances.
-	Hand dimensions.
-	Eye level.
-	Working height.
-	Leg clearance.
-	Grip diameter.
-	Accessible operating positions.

Important: 

!!! Info "Important"

    Anthropometric information should come from relevant sources. Do not use arbitrary measurements.

Consider the range of people expected to use the product, rather than designing only for an average user.

You may need to investigate percentile data, adjustability or inclusive design principles.

**Turning research into decisions**

- Research finding: Users vary considerably in body dimensions.
- Design implication: A fixed dimension may not accommodate every intended user.
- Potential response: Investigate adjustability or select a dimension appropriate to the target user group, supported by relevant data.

## 2.7 Research materials and components

At A-Level, material selection should be based on properties and performance.

Depending on your project, investigate:

<div class="grid cards" markdown>

- **Tensile Strength**
- **Compressive Strength**
- **Stiffness**
- **Toughness**
- **Hardness**
- **Density**
- **Flexibility**
- **Corrosion Resistance**
- **Wear Resistance**
- **Thermal Properties**
- **Electrical Properties**
- **Workability**
- **Surface Finish**
- **Availability and Cost**

</div>

Not every property will be relevant to every product.

Select the properties that matter for the intended function.

!!! note "Example"
    | Factor | Material A | Material B | Decision to Investigate |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | **Density** | Lower | Higher | Does reduced mass improve portability? |
    | **Stiffness** | Moderate | Higher | What level of rigidity is required? |
    | **Corrosion Resistance** | Good | Requires protection | Will the product be exposed to moisture? |
    | **Cost** | Higher | Lower | Is the performance benefit worth the cost? |
    | **Manufacture** | Requires suitable joining methods | Can use alternative processes | Which approach is achievable and effective? |

!!! warning "Outer Note"
    Do not choose a material simply because it is available in the workshop. Explain why it is appropriate for the client and design requirements.

## 2.8 Investigate manufacturing processes

Research processes that could realistically be used for your product.

Consider:
<div class="grid cards" markdown>

- **Accuracy and Tolerances**
- **Material Compatibility**
- **Equipment Requirements**
- **Skill Level**
- **Surface Quality**
- **Production Speed**
- **Cost**
- **Wastage**
- **Repeatability**
- **Environmental Impact**

</div>

You may need to compare manual and computer-controlled methods.
For example, a CNC process may improve repeatability, while a manually manufactured component may allow greater opportunity to demonstrate practical shaping and fitting skills.

Your final selection should depend on the component and the manufacturing requirement.

## 2.9 Investigate standards and safety requirements

Where relevant, identify recognised standards, technical guidance or safety considerations associated with your proposed product.

**Consider:**
-	Product stability.
-	Mechanical strength.
-	Electrical safety.
-	Material suitability.
-	Sharp edges and moving components.
-	Accessibility.
-	Safe operation.

Only reference standards that genuinely apply to your design.
If a standard gives a particular requirement, ensure you have consulted an authoritative source before applying it.

## 2.10 Investigate production scale
The delivery guide expects consideration of production levels.

**Ask:**
-	Is this a bespoke product?
-	Would it be manufactured in a small batch?
-	Could it be produced commercially?
-	Which processes would be appropriate at different production volumes?
-	How would tooling and labour costs change?
-	Could standard components reduce cost?
-	Could the design be simplified for assembly?

Your NEA prototype is likely to be a one-off, but you should demonstrate an awareness of how production scale affects design decisions.

## 2.11 Investigate sustainability across the product life cycle

**Consider the following stages:**
1.	Raw material extraction.
2.	Material processing.
3.	Component manufacture.
4.	Assembly.
5.	Packaging and transportation.
6.	Use and maintenance.
7.	Repair and replacement.
8.	End-of-life treatment.

Avoid making unsupported claims such as "wood is always environmentally friendly" or "plastic is always unsustainable".

The impact depends on the specific material, source, manufacturing method, lifespan and disposal route.

A durable product that can be repaired may perform differently across its life cycle from one that requires frequent replacement.

## 2.12 Create a research conclusion

At the end of your research, summarise the findings that will influence your specification.

!!! example "Example"
    | Important Finding | Evidence | Implication for the Design |
    |---|---|---|
    | **Storage space is limited** | Client interview and measurements | The design should minimise its dimensions when stored. |
    | **The product may encounter moisture** | Observation of use environment | Materials and finishes must be appropriate. |
    | **Users require comfortable seating** | Client feedback and ergonomic research | Dimensions and support should be informed by user data. |
    | **The product must be moved regularly** | Client investigation | Mass and handling features need consideration. |

This is one of the most useful ways to demonstrate that your research has a purpose.

**Common weaknesses to avoid**
-	Producing generic ACCESSFM pages without relevant analysis.
-	Copying information from websites.
-	Analysing products only by describing their appearance.
-	Using material research that is unrelated to the final design.
-	Making unsupported statements about sustainability.
-	Including measurements without sources.
-	Failing to explain how research influences your decisions.
