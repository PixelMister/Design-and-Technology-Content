# Case Hardening

## What is Case Hardening?
Case hardening is a **heat treatment process** used mainly on **steel** to create a **hard outer surface (case)** while keeping a **tough, ductile core**. This combination allows a component to resist **wear and abrasion** on the surface while also withstanding **impact and shock**.

In Product Design, case hardening is important for components that experience **friction on the outside but need strength and toughness internally**.

---

## Why Case Hardening is Used
Case hardening is used to:
- Increase surface hardness
- Improve wear resistance
- Maintain a tough core
- Extend component lifespan
- Prevent brittle failure

It is commonly used for **moving mechanical parts**.

---

## How Case Hardening Works
Case hardening works by **adding carbon or nitrogen to the surface of low‑carbon steel**, followed by heating and cooling.

### Basic Principle:
- Only the **outer layer** is hardened
- The **core remains softer and tougher**
- Produces a balance of hardness and toughness

---

## Main Case Hardening Processes

### Carburising
- Steel is heated in a **carbon‑rich environment**
- Carbon diffuses into the surface
- Component is then quenched
- Produces a very hard outer layer

### Nitriding
- Nitrogen is added to the surface
- Lower temperatures than carburising
- No quenching required
- Produces a hard, wear‑resistant surface

### Induction Hardening
- Uses **electromagnetic induction**
- Only the surface is heated rapidly
- Followed by rapid cooling
- Precise and fast process

### Cyaniding (less common)
- Uses carbon and nitrogen compounds
- Very fast but toxic
- Rarely used today due to safety concerns

---

## Effects of Case Hardening
After case hardening:
- ✅ Surface hardness increases significantly
- ✅ Wear resistance improves
- ✅ Core remains tough and shock‑resistant
- ❌ Process is complex
- ❌ Limited to suitable steels

---

## Case Hardening vs Through Hardening
| Feature | Case Hardening | Through Hardening |
|------|----------------|------------------|
| Hardness | Surface only | Entire component |
| Core | Tough | Hard and brittle |
| Wear resistance | High | High |
| Impact resistance | High | Lower |
| Typical use | Gears, shafts | Cutting tools |

---

## Key Properties Affected by Case Hardening
| Property | Effect |
|--------|-------|
| Surface Hardness | Increases |
| Wear Resistance | Increases |
| Core Toughness | Maintained |
| Brittleness | Reduced compared to through hardening |
| Durability | Improved |

---

## Advantages of Case Hardening
- Hard, wear‑resistant surface
- Tough core resists impact
- Longer service life
- Reduced risk of cracking
- Ideal for moving parts

---

## Disadvantages of Case Hardening
- Expensive and time‑consuming
- Requires specialist equipment
- Limited to certain steels
- Difficult to recycle
- Not suitable for simple components

---

## Typical Uses in Product Design
Case hardened components include:
- **Gears**
- **Camshafts**
- **Shafts**
- **Bearings**
- **Pins**
- **Automotive components**
- **Machine parts**

---

## Materials Suitable for Case Hardening
- Low‑carbon steel ✅
- Mild steel ✅
- Alloy steel ✅
- Stainless steel ❌ (generally unsuitable)
- Aluminium ❌
- Copper ❌

---

## Health and Safety Considerations
- High temperatures involved
- Risk of burns
- Hazardous gases in some processes
- Industrial process only
- Not suitable for school workshop manufacture

---

## Case Hardening Compared to Other Heat Treatments
| Process | Main Purpose |
|-------|--------------|
| Hardening | Increase hardness throughout |
| Tempering | Reduce brittleness |
| Annealing | Soften metal |
| Case Hardening | Hard surface, tough core |

---

## Suitability for Product Design
Case hardening is suitable when:
- Surface wear is significant
- Components experience friction
- Impact resistance is required
- Long service life is essential

Case hardening is not suitable when:
- Low cost is essential
- Simple manufacturing is required
- Non‑ferrous metals are used
- Uniform hardness is needed

## Overall Summary
Case hardening is a **heat treatment process** that produces a **hard, wear‑resistant outer surface** while maintaining a **tough and ductile core**. This combination makes it ideal for components such as **gears, shafts, and bearings**, which must resist friction and wear without becoming brittle. Although case hardening is **complex and expensive**, it significantly improves **durability and performance**. In A Level Product Design, case hardening should be evaluated by considering its **effect on surface hardness, core toughness, manufacturing complexity, safety, and suitability for high‑wear mechanical components**.
