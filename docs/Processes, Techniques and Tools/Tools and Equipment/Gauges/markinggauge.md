# Marking Gauge

## What Is a Marking Gauge?
A marking gauge is a **specialist marking out tool used mainly in woodworking** to mark **accurate lines parallel to an edge** of a piece of timber. It produces a **fine, precise line** that does not rub off easily, making it ideal for **joint preparation and accurate cutting**.

In A Level Product Design, the marking gauge is a key tool for **precision marking**, especially when producing **traditional wood joints**.

---

## Main Parts of a Marking Gauge
A standard marking gauge consists of three main parts:

### Stock (Fence)
- Flat wooden or plastic block
- Rests against the edge of the timber

**Purpose:**
- Keeps the gauge aligned
- Ensures the line is parallel to the edge

---

### Beam (Stem)
- Long shaft that slides through the stock
- Usually wooden or metal

**Purpose:**
- Sets the distance from the edge
- Controls the position of the marking line

---

### Spur / Pin
- Sharp metal point at the end of the beam

**Purpose:**
- Scores a fine line into the wood
- More accurate than a pencil
- Reduces tear‑out when cutting

---

## How a Marking Gauge Is Used
1. Measure the required distance using a steel rule
2. Adjust the beam so the spur is set to that distance
3. Lock the beam in place (if adjustable)
4. Hold the stock firmly against the face edge
5. Pull the marking gauge along the wood
6. The spur scratches a **fine, accurate line**

The line produced is **parallel to the reference edge**.

---

## Why a Marking Gauge Is More Accurate Than a Pencil
| Feature | Marking Gauge | Pencil |
|------|--------------|--------|
| Line thickness | Very thin | Thick |
| Accuracy | Very high | Medium |
| Rubbing off | No | Yes |
| Joint marking | Excellent | Poor |

A marking gauge is preferred for **precision work**.

---

## Types of Marking Gauge

### Single‑Pin Marking Gauge
- One spur
- Most common type

**Used for:**
- General marking out
- Parallel lines
- Joint shoulders

---

### Mortise Gauge
- Two adjustable spurs
- A specialist form of marking gauge

**Used for:**
- Marking mortise and tenon joints
- Ensuring both parts match exactly

---

### Cutting Gauge
- Uses a small blade instead of a pin

**Used for:**
- Across the grain
- Preventing splintering
- Very fine joinery

---

## Typical Uses of a Marking Gauge
A marking gauge is used for:

- **Mortise and tenon joints**
- **Housing joints**
- **Lap joints**
- **Shoulder lines**
- **Parallel layout lines**
- **Accurate joint preparation**

It is essential in **traditional woodworking and furniture making**.

---

## Best Practice When Using a Marking Gauge
- Always mark from the **face side or face edge**
- Pull the gauge **towards you**, not away
- Apply light, consistent pressure
- Keep the spur sharp
- Mark lightly at first, then deepen if needed
- Clearly mark the waste side

---

## Health and Safety Considerations
- The spur is sharp – keep fingers clear
- Store the gauge safely when not in use
- Do not apply excessive force
- Ensure timber is supported or clamped
- Keep the spur retracted when carrying

Good technique reduces the risk of slips and injury.

---

## Marking Gauge Compared to Other Marking Tools
| Tool | Best Use | Accuracy |
|----|--------|----------|
| Marking Gauge | Parallel lines | Very High |
| Mortise Gauge | Joint widths | Very High |
| Pencil | General marking | Medium |
| Marking Knife | Joint lines | Very High |

The marking gauge is **unique** for marking **parallel lines**.

---

## Suitability for A Level Product Design
The marking gauge is suitable when:
- High accuracy is required
- Traditional wood joints are used
- Components must fit precisely
- Professional‑quality marking is expected

It is less suitable when:
- Rough layout work is required
- Materials are not wood
- Speed is prioritised over precision

## Overall Summary
A marking gauge is a **precision marking out tool used in woodworking** to produce **accurate lines parallel to an edge**. Consisting of a **stock, beam, and sharp spur**, it allows designers to mark joints and layout lines far more accurately than a pencil. It is especially important for **mortise and tenon joints and other traditional wood joints**, where precision is essential. In A Level Product Design, the marking gauge should be understood as a **specialist marking out tool** that improves **accuracy, quality, and safety**, forming a fundamental part of professional woodworking practice.
